Research Background

After giving a thorough thought on the concept of involuntary loops and chosen thresholds, I came to realize these around me in my everyday life. It wasn’t because I had a lot of these activities around me, but it was more because I started to be conscious about them. I wondered what others would find within their life that feel repetitive, and what emotions and thoughts emerge from them. To build consciousness and awareness to look for these loops, I thought it would be best to ask people to log activities in a regular basis, thus led me to employ a diary study method for my first research.

Research Plan

Step 1. Self-observation prompt

For the next 3 days, write down at least 3 things you notice yourself repeating unconsciously. It could be physical (tapping, doodling), digital (refreshing apps, checking notifications), and even an activity you perform daily (drinking coffee, going for a walk). Categorize it by either routine or habit and tag emotions (ex. calming, surprised, absurd) and write a paragraph of thoughts doing or after realizing your repetitive action.

Research Log

13 people were asked to participate. 10 participants accurately logged their activities according to the prompt and a total of 79 activities were logged.

Interesting findings

Future improvements

After answering questions during the research, I realized I could’ve specified the prompt better. The most frequently asked questions was whether or not they could log the same activity multiple times. Although it would be best to keep new activities, it was equally important to see the difference in emotion or thoughts after acknowledging their habits or routines. Just an additional text explaining that would’ve been great.

Another aspect to improve would be the recording of category(routine or habit). Since I’ve narrowed the category to these two, participants were prevented from thinking beyond the two. The term routine and habit also overlapped in someway, and also made participants think of the same activity in between days. Removing the category could differ the content logged.

Asking participants and explaining what to do in person seemed to be more effective in addressing my research. People who were asked in person, either offline or online, were found to be more accurately logging their activities.

Reflection

I did spend some time coming up with this research activity, and there wasn’t so much time left to execute. I might be able to find a different pattern, or even different questions if the logging was executed for more than a week. Ideally, it could’ve put the participants in habit of finding these activities, making them more consciously think about the topic. I would like to develop this method and try it again for a longer period, with an improved setting.
